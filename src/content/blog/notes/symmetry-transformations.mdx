## Scalar Fields

Suppose I have a hot cup of coffee described by a temperature field $\phi(\bs{x})$, and I slide the cup by a displacement $\bs{a}$ on my desk. If physics is going to be of any use in this world, then the total heat content of the coffee better have not changed. Why?

Because space is *homogeneous* -- there is no preferred position. In other words, the energy $F[\phi]$ of the coffee cup is invariant under actions from the translation group $\mathbb{R}^3$. How do we describe the transformed temperature field of this displaced coffee? 
<Figure
  svg={fieldTranslation}
  width={420}
  alt="A coffee cup at position r, and the same cup displaced by a vector a to a new position, with temperature fields labeled phi and phi-prime"
  caption="Displacing the cup by $\bs{a}$ carries the field $\phi$ to a new field $\phi'$."
/>

Evidently, the transformed temperature field $\phi'$ now has support in a different region of space. This is a whole new function, it is an entirely different configuration of the coffee cup. In other words, $\phi$ is *not* invariant under translations, unlike the energy. This new function is defined by

$$
\begin{align*}
    \phi'(\bs{x} + \bs{a}) = \phi(\bs{x}).
\end{align*}
$$
This is the most straightforward way to describe the transformation, however, it is customary (and also useful) to describe the transformed field from *its* perspective. Letting $\bs{x}' = \bs{x} + \bs{a}$, we get

$$
\begin{align}
    \htmlId{eq:scalar-translation}{
      \phi'(\bs{x}') = \phi(\bs{x}) 
      = \phi(\bs{x}' - \bs{a}).
    }
\end{align}
$$
In general, if $g$ is an element of a group $G$, then the transformed scalar field $g \cdot \phi$ is 
$$
\begin{align}
  \htmlId{eq:scalar-transform}
  (g \cdot \phi)(\bs{x}) = \phi(g^{-1} \bs{x}).
\end{align}
$$
If the inverse group element is ever confusing, just remember that it comes from expressing the field and points in terms of the new reference frame as in Eq. [](#eq:scalar-translation). This is called a *pullback* -- we define the transformed field $\phi'$ by pulling the evaluation point $\bs{x}$ back to its original position $g^{-1}\bs{x}$ and evaluate the *old* function there.

## Vector Fields

Now, let's consider a bar magnet whose local magnetization is described by a vector field $\bs{m}(\bs{x})$. In the absence of any external field, I can rotate this magnet by some transformation matrix $R$, and the energy of the magnetic field better not change. Why?

<SideNote label="aside"> For relativistic systems, the fundamental group of symmetries is the Poincaré group $P(1,3)$, which is the combination of spacetime translations $\mathbb{R}^{(1,3)}$ and spacetime rotations $O(1,3)$.</SideNote>

Because in addition to being homogeneous, space is also *isotropic* -- there is no preferred direction. That means the energy of the bar magnet $F[\bs{m}]$ is invariant under translations $\mathbb{R}^3$ as well under the group of rotations $O(3)$. Together, these transformations make up the *Euclidean* group $E(3)$, the fundamental group of symmetries for flat three-dimensional space. Let's see how our magnetic field transforms under a rotation:

<Figure
  svg={fieldVectorTransform}
  width={560}
  alt="A bar magnet with magnetization m and position vector x, and three attempts to rotate it by R: rotating both x and m gives the correct transformed field; rotating only m or only x each leaves the magnetization misaligned with the bar and is marked wrong."
  caption="To transform a vector field, both the positions $\bs{x}$ and the vectors $\bs{m}$ must rotate together (bottom right). Rotating only one leaves the magnetization misaligned with the bar."
/>

From the diagram above, we can see that the transformed field $\bs{m}'$ is described by,

$$
\begin{align*}
  \bs{m}'(R\bs{x}) 
  = R\bs{m}(\bs{x}).
\end{align*}
$$
Letting $\bs{x}' = R \bs{x}$, we can again express this as a pullback function,
$$
\begin{align}
  \htmlId{eq:vector-rotation}{
  \bs{m}'(\bs{x}') 
  = R\bs{m}(R^{-1}\bs{x}') }
\end{align}
$$
so that the rotated field is defined entirely in terms of the untransformed field. This reveals an important distinction between symmetry transformations on scalar fields (Eq. [](#eq:scalar-transform)) and on vector fields: for vector fields, one must transform both the inputs *and* the outputs. From Eq. [](#eq:vector-rotation), it may appear that we should just transform the vector field opposite how we transform the base space, however, we can come up with an immediate counterexample. 

Consider now a general rigid-body transformations $\bs{x}' = R\bs{x} + \bs{a}$. Shifting the magnet by a translation should certainly not change the direction of the field, so only the rotation operator should act on $\bs{m}$, giving
$$
\bs{m}'(\bs{x}') = R \bs{m}(R^{-1}(\bs{x}' - \bs{a})).
$$

We require the value of the transformed field to match, which we verify below.

<Derivation label="Derivation: Jacobian">
Start from the transformation rule and apply the chain rule. Referencing an

new relation inside the block,

$$
\begin{align}
  \htmlId{eq:jacobian}{ J_{ij} = \frac{\partial x'_i}{\partial x_j} = R_{ij} }
\end{align}
$$

we can refer back to Eq [](#eq:jacobian) from inside the derivation. This block

The Jacobian defined in the derivation above (Eq [](#eq:jacobian)) is just the
rotation matrix itself.

## Tensor Fields

The previous section motivates the transformation rule for general tensor fields. 

For a general group element $g$, vector fields transform as

$$
(g \cdot T)(\bs{x}) = \rho(J(\bs{x})) T(g^{-1}\bs{r})
$$

where $\rho(g)$ is the representation of $g$ in the space of $\bs{m}$. For instance, for general rigid body motions $(\bs{a}, R)$, 

$$
\begin{align}
(g \cdot Q)(\bs{x}) = \rho(g) Q(g^{-1} \bs{x})
\end{align}
$$
