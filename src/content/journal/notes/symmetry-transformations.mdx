## Scalar Fields

Suppose I have a hot cup of coffee described by a temperature field $\phi(\bs{x})$, and I slide the cup by a displacement $\bs{a}$ on my desk. If physics is going to be of any use to us, then the total heat content of the coffee better not have changed. Why must this be?

Because space is *homogeneous* -- there is no preferred or privileged position. Mathematically, the energy $F[\phi]$ of the coffee cup is invariant under actions from the translation group $\mathbb{R}^3$. How do we describe the transformed temperature field of this displaced coffee? 
<Figure
  svg={fieldTranslation}
  width={420}
  alt="A coffee cup at position r, and the same cup displaced by a vector a to a new position, with temperature fields labeled phi and phi-prime"
  caption="Displacing the cup by $\bs{a}$ carries the field $\phi$ to a new field $\phi'$."
/>

Evidently, the transformed temperature field $\phi'$ is now has support in a different region of space. This is a whole new function, it is an entirely different configuration of the coffee cup. The function $\phi$ is *not* invariant under translations, unlike the energy. This new function is defined by

$$
\begin{align*}
    \phi'(\bs{x} + \bs{a}) = \phi(\bs{x}).
\end{align*}
$$
This is the most straightforward way to describe the transformation, however, it is customary (and also useful) to describe the transformed field from *its* perspective. Letting $\bs{x}' = \bs{x} + \bs{a}$, we get

$$
\begin{align}
    \htmlId{eq:scalar-translation}{
      \phi'(\bs{x}') = \phi(\bs{x}) 
      = \phi(\bs{x}' - \bs{a}).
    }
\end{align}
$$
In general, if $g$ is an element of a group $G$, then the transformed scalar field $g \cdot \phi$ is 
$$
\begin{align}
  \htmlId{eq:scalar-transform}
  (g \cdot \phi)(\bs{x}) = \phi(g^{-1} \bs{x}).
\end{align}
$$
If the inverse group element is ever confusing, just remember that it comes from expressing the field and points in terms of the new reference frame as in Eq. [](#eq:scalar-translation). This is called a *pullback* -- we define the transformed field $\phi'$ by pulling the evaluation point $\bs{x}$ back to its original position $g^{-1}\bs{x}$ and evaluate the *old* function there.

## Vector Fields

Now, let's consider a bar magnet whose local magnetization is described by a vector field $\bs{m}(\bs{x})$. In the absence of any external field, I can rotate this magnet by rotation matrix $R$, and the energy of the magnetic field should not change. Why must this be?



Because in addition to being homogeneous, space is also *isotropic* -- there is no preferred or privileged direction. That means the energy of the bar magnet $F[\bs{m}]$ is not only invariant under translations $\mathbb{R}^3$, but also under the group of rotations $O(3)$. Together, these transformations make up the *Euclidean* group $E(3)$, the fundamental group of symmetries for flat three-dimensional space. <SideNote label="aside"> For relativistic systems, the fundamental group of symmetries is the Poincaré group $P(1,3)$, which is the combination of spacetime translations $\mathbb{R}^{(1,3)}$ and spacetime rotations $O(1,3)$.</SideNote> Let's see how our magnetic field transforms under a rotation:

<Figure
  svg={fieldVectorTransform}
  width={560}
  alt="A bar magnet with magnetization m and position vector x, and three attempts to rotate it by R: rotating both x and m gives the correct transformed field; rotating only m or only x each leaves the magnetization misaligned with the bar and is marked wrong."
  caption="To transform a vector field, both the positions $\bs{x}$ and the vectors $\bs{m}$ must rotate together (bottom right). Rotating only one leaves the magnetization misaligned with the bar."
/>

From the diagram above, we can see that the transformed field $\bs{m}'$ is described by,

$$
\begin{align*}
  \bs{m}'(R\bs{x}) 
  = R\bs{m}(\bs{x}).
\end{align*}
$$
Letting $\bs{x}' = R \bs{x}$, we can again express this as a pullback function,
$$
\begin{align}
  \htmlId{eq:vector-rotation}{
  \bs{m}'(\bs{x}') 
  = R\bs{m}(R^{-1}\bs{x}') }
\end{align}
$$
so that the rotated field is defined entirely in terms of the untransformed field. This reveals an important distinction between symmetry transformations on scalar fields (Eq. [](#eq:scalar-transform)) and on vector fields: for vector fields, one must transform both the inputs *and* the outputs. From Eq. [](#eq:vector-rotation), it may appear that we should just transform the vector field opposite how we transform the base space, however, we can come up with an immediate counterexample. 

Consider now a general rigid-body transformation $\bs{x}' = R\bs{x} + \bs{a}$. Translating the magnet should certainly not change the direction of the field, so only the rotation operator should act on $\bs{m}$, giving
$$
\bs{m}'(\bs{x}') = R \bs{m}(R^{-1}(\bs{x}' - \bs{a})).
$$
So, we see that the transformation acting on the vector field $\bs{m}$ is not simply the rigid body motion $g$. The proper transformation is given by the *Jacobian* of the rigid body motion $J(\bs{x}) = R$. The Jacobian may generally vary over space, but it is constant in this simple example. 

<Derivation label="Derivation: Why the Jacobian shows up">
Let $\Phi$ be an arbitrary coordinate transformation (an invertible, differentiable function) and let $\bs{v}$ represent a velocity field. Choose an arbitrary point $\bs{x}$ and define the curve $\gamma(t)$ such that $\gamma(0) = \bs{x}$ and $\dot{\gamma}(0) = \bs{v}(\bs{x})$ -- the curve passes through the chosen point and is tangent to the streamline through that point at $t = 0$. 

<Figure
  svg={velocityTransform}
  width={550}
  alt="Left: a curved velocity field v, with a curve gamma of t that cuts across the neighbouring streamlines but runs tangent to the one through the point x, where its tangent vector is v(x). An arrow labelled Phi above and J of x below carries this to the right panel: a straightened field v-prime in which the image curve Phi of gamma of t is likewise tangent at x-prime, with tangent vector v-prime(x-prime)."
  caption="The curve $\gamma(t)$ need not follow the field, but it is tangent to it at $\bs{x}$ — so its velocity there is exactly $\bs{v}(\bs{x})$. Carrying the curve through $\Phi$ and differentiating at $t=0$ sends that tangent vector to $\bs{v}'(\bs{x}')$, so the field is carried by the Jacobian rather than by $\Phi$ itself."
/>

We require that the transformed field $\bs{v}'(\bs{x}')$ be equal to the velocity of the transformed curve $\Phi(\gamma(t))$. Calculating the velocity of the transformed curve, we are left with

$$
\begin{align}
\bs{v}'(\bs{x}')
= \frac{d}{dt} \Phi(\gamma(t))\; \Big|_{t = 0}
= \frac{d\Phi}{dx}
\frac{d\gamma}{dt} \Big|_{t = 0}
= J(\bs{x}) \bs{v}(\bs{x})
\end{align}
$$

where we have identified the derivative of the coordinate map as the Jacobian, $J$, at the point $\bs{x}$.

</Derivation>

## Tensor Fields

The previous section motivates the transformation rule for general tensor fields. For a general group element $g$, a tensor field $T$ transforms as

$$
(g \cdot T)(\bs{x}) = \rho(J(\bs{x})) T(g^{-1}\bs{x})
$$

where $\rho$ is the representation of $g$ in the space of $T$ and $J$ is the Jacobian. As an example, let us consider the Cauchy stress, a $(2,0)$ tensor.
